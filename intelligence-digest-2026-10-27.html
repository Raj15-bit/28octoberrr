The big picture: foreign money is giving up on India just as Indian profits jump

The world is pulling money out of Indian shares, while Indian companies report their fastest profit growth in years.

In short
  • Foreign investors now hold India at about half the weight they did 2 years ago.
  • At the same time, the typical listed company grew its profit by about 30%.
  • So the fight is between a price problem (tax, rates, rupee) and a profit story that keeps getting better.

The material splits into 2 stories that do not fit together. One is about money leaving: foreign funds, a weak rupee, a rate rise and tax. The other is about business: orders, profits and new factories. The map below puts each force on 1 tile.

The deepest foreign gloom in 30 years

Akash Prakash of Amansa Capital wrote that in 30 years of investing he has rarely seen foreign feeling towards India this bad. Fund managers who stayed heavy on India lost their 10-year records in 24 months. He reads that as a sign the selling is late, not early.

  • Weight in the index — India's share of the MSCI emerging markets index fell from 21% in September 2024 to about 10%.
  • Against peers — India is down about 25% in dollars from its peak while emerging markets rose about 50%.
  • Asian chips — Taiwan, Korea and China now make up about 70% of the index; 3 Asian tech companies alone weigh about as much as India and China together.
  • The tax point — allocators are upset about India's capital gains tax; section 02 covers the push to cut it.

One view is that this much pain marks a bottom, because the sellers who had to sell have mostly sold. The opposite view is that India's weight fell because chip companies grew, not because India failed, so the money may not come back even if India does well. Reading between the lines, India now competes with the AI chip boom for the same foreign dollar.

How the pieces connect

The common thread is the price of money. War keeps oil high, oil keeps inflation high, and inflation made the Reserve Bank of India raise rates. Higher US borrowing costs, pushed up partly by AI companies borrowing record sums, pull dollars away. A weak rupee then cuts what a foreign investor earns. Profits are rising, but foreign investors judge India in dollars after tax.

STEP 1War near Hormuz keeps Brent near $115 a barrel
STEP 2Dear fuel lifts inflation; RBI raises the repo rate to 5.50%
STEP 3US rates and AI borrowing pull dollars to the US; the rupee averages 95
STEP 4Tax plus a weak rupee cut a foreign investor's dollar return
STEP 5Foreign funds sell even as company profits grow about 30%

So the turn, when it comes, may come from a tax change or a cheaper barrel rather than from better results, because the results are already good. Section 10 sets out both sides of whether the fall is nearly over.

02

Price of money: India tightens, and asks whether tax is driving investors away

A rate rise at home, high borrowing costs abroad, and a loud call to cut the tax on share gains.

In short
  • India's central bank raised its main rate to 5.50%, its first rise since early 2023.
  • Many big investors want the tax on long-term share gains cut or removed for foreigners.
  • Ideas to limit how much money Indians can send abroad are being discussed, but none is confirmed.

The rate rise and what banks feel

On 7 October the Reserve Bank of India raised the repo rate (the rate at which it lends to banks) by 0.25% to 5.50%. All 6 members voted for it, and the stance moved to "calibrated tightening", which means more rises are possible.

Lenders are already reacting. Shriram Finance said the change in the rate cycle makes a large fall in its own borrowing cost unlikely. Aavas Financiers is passing the rise on to borrowers. The head of Axis Bank warned of tighter cash in the system, dearer deposits and pressure on how much banks earn on each loan.

The dollar-deposit carry trade

Banks gathered about $133 billion through a special window for FCNR deposits (dollar deposits from Indians living abroad), about 5 times the 2013 window. Much of it was a loop, shown in the pointers below.

  • The loop — an NRI puts in $100, the bank's overseas unit lends $900, and the whole $1,000 goes into the deposit, pledged against the loan.
  • Why banks did it — the RBI swapped the dollars for cheap rupees, which banks could lend at about 9%.
  • The cost — these are fixed deposits, so they dilute cheap current and savings money; a 6.8% cost was cited for IDFC First Bank.
  • When it ends — the deposits mature in mid-2029.

Reading between the lines, part of the fast deposit growth at banks is this one-time loop, not new savings. The window had to close before the rate rise could make sense.

The tax on share gains

A long list of well-known investors says the long-term capital gains tax (12.5% on gains over ₹1.25 lakh from shares held more than 1 year) is now a main reason foreigners stay away. The finance minister said on 13 October she would hear views, and reports say a decision could come within weeks.

One view is that removing the tax for foreigners alone would still help, and waiting for the budget could cost another $30 billion of outflows. The opposite view is that India changes tax rules too often already, and markets need rules that stay put more than a lower rate. So the timing of any change matters as much as its size.

Limits on money going abroad

A television report said the RBI asked the government to make investment outflows under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS, which lets each Indian send up to $250,000 a year abroad) need its permission, with a ₹50 lakh cap on other spending. Another claim said the limit could fall to zero. Neither could be confirmed from an official source.

  • What is on record — SBI Research on 15 October asked the RBI and government to curb non-essential LRS outflows and look closely at Indian firms' investment abroad.
  • Size — Indians sent nearly $29bn under LRS in FY26; resident firms' investment abroad was about $27.8bn.
  • Older idea — reports in May already spoke of halving the limit for a while.

Reading between the lines, the 2 ideas pull the same way: cut tax so foreign money comes in, and limit Indian money going out. Both defend the rupee. One view is that the rich are now moving dollars out, so foreign funds are not the only sellers. The opposite view is that capital controls scare the very foreign investors India wants back.

Rates abroad

The US Federal Reserve raised its rate to 3.75%-4.00% in September and was expected to hold on 28 October, with a December rise still priced. Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan both expect a hold, with JPMorgan expecting some members to vote for a rise.

  • US 2-year auction — foreign buyers took 45.7%, the least since November 2021.
  • Weakest borrowers — US CCC-rated junk bonds yield over 16%, the first time since 2020.
  • Bank of Canada — expected to raise its rate to 2.50%, its first rise since July 2023.
  • Japan — the Bank of Japan's core inflation measure rose to 3.2%, a 3-year high.
  • Europe — ECB survey: banks tightened lending as demand for loans fell.

Dave McKay, chief of the Royal Bank of Canada, called long-term yields (what lenders demand to lend for many years) the biggest risk, because both government deficits and the AI boom need huge borrowing. So the AI boom in the US and India's rate pain are linked through the same pool of savings.

03

Oil and war: peace talk lowers the price a little, but the ships tell another story

Iran offers a plan, Saudi Arabia ships at full speed, and Europe heads into winter short of gas.

In short
  • Iran's leader spoke of peace terms and oil slipped for 2 days, but stayed far above $100.
  • A record amount of oil is sitting on ships, because routes are longer and risky.
  • India earns more from selling diesel abroad than ever, even while selling less of it.

Talks, a plan, and more attacks

Saudi Arabia's defence minister flew to Tehran with Pakistan's army chief and Turkey's intelligence head. Iran then floated a 7-day plan: the Strait of Hormuz reopens on day 6 if the US accepts its terms, and talks start on day 7. Supreme leader Mojtaba Khamenei called for pursuing Iran's conditions to end the war.

  • Ras Tanura — Saudi Arabia put out a fire near its main oil port after a Houthi attack on a refinery; an earlier attack killed 2 contractors near a big oilfield.
  • Hormuz — a tanker was disabled by a projectile on 23 October, and another caught fire after a mine; an oil spill off the UAE coast is widening.
  • US forces — reported ready for major action against Iran within 96 hours of an order.
  • Iran — says it shot down a US drone, but also says it is ready to back any deal for regional unity.
  • Qatar — says Iran must stop threats to Gulf states and open Hormuz for talks to work.

One view is that the meeting changes nothing: the war resumes after the US midterm vote on 3 November and the Israeli count, and the next risk window runs from 6 November to early January. The opposite view is cautious hope, since Iran showed rare flexibility and oil fell 2 days in a row. The market is not pricing a new war: WTI is near $101 while the risks stay the same.

Reading between the lines, Saudi Arabia is selling as much as it can while the route is open. Its exports are back above 7 million barrels a day through both the Gulf and the Red Sea. That is a bet that the calm is short.

The oil is on the water, and shipping is the cost

Oil in transit hit a record of about 1.3 billion barrels, because ships take longer routes and swap cargo at sea. A Korean shipping group, Sinokor, was reported hiring tankers at very high rates, and Iran started legal action against its vessels in Hormuz.

  • Price shape — the gap between December and January Brent futures widened to $5.11, which means buyers pay a lot more for oil now than later.
  • Refineries — Goldman Sachs said refinery outages are worse than its worst case, with weak supply into 2027.
  • Outlook — Fatih Birol of the IEA sees no clear path back to pre-war energy markets in 2027.
  • US escort — the US Army says it helped move 1.5bn barrels out of Hormuz, escorting more than 3,000 ships.

India is now the world's 2nd-largest seaborne diesel exporter, behind the US. Reliance Industries ships about 70% of it, with Nayara Energy and MRPL next, and state refiners plan to add more diesel output in 2027. So India gains as a refiner even as it pays more as a crude importer.

  • The other side — India still imports about 90% of its crude.

Europe's winter problem

Europe's gas storage is about 75% full against a 10-year norm of about 90%, and a cold snap pushed gas prices up. France is hit hardest, with dear diesel and street unrest that led to many arrests.

  • Gas — a cold snap pushed the Dutch gas price near €97 per MWh.
  • France at the pump — diesel costs €2.37 a litre; police made about 1,300 arrests in unrest.
  • French borrowing — the extra yield France pays over Germany rose from 0.75% to 1.72%; insurance on French debt more than doubled.
  • French shoppers — consumer confidence fell to 80, a 13-year low.
  • Fertiliser — urea prices in France are up about 60%.

Elections and new routes

  • Israel — voted on 27 October; Benjamin Netanyahu ran neck and neck with Gadi Eisenkot, after a public clash with army chief Eyal Zamir over action on Iran.
  • Northern Sea Route — an Arctic sea route from Russia to India could cut the trip from 40 to 24 days; pilot voyages are planned for 2027.
  • Why it matters — more than 60% of India's energy imports pass through Hormuz or the Red Sea.
  • Other fronts — Algeria sent troops into Niger to guard a gas pipeline, its first foreign deployment since 1973.

So India is looking for routes that avoid both choke points, which tells you officials expect the risk to last for years.

Borrowed money forced out

Many small investors buy shares with a broker loan, called margin trading (MTF). When prices fall, brokers sell the shares to recover the loan. On Friday 23 October more than ₹5,400 crore of such positions were sold and smaller companies fell about 3%.

  • Week's total — net cut of ₹4,450cr in borrowed positions, the biggest weekly fall since January 2017.
  • What is left — the margin book on NSE is still about ₹1.44 lakh cr.
  • A poll — in a poll of more than 500 people, about 25% of small investors said they use borrowed money.
  • Foreign bets — foreign funds hold only 13 bets on a rise for every 87 bets on a fall in index futures.

Reading between the lines, the sharp bounce in smaller companies on 26 and 27 October came after forced sellers were cleared out, not after any new good news. With foreign funds this one-sided, even a small piece of good news can force them to buy back.

India's economy: the state spends hard, while farms and shoppers slow

Dry fields and falling card spends on one side; defence orders, trade deals and building on the other.

In short
  • About half of India's land was abnormally dry, and farm loans are turning bad.
  • Credit card spending fell by the most on record.
  • The government keeps spending on defence, roads and rail, and a trade deal with Europe is close.

Rural stress: dry land and bad farm loans

The drought monitor for 23 September showed 51.2% of India's area as abnormally dry or worse. Shriram Finance named Maharashtra, Karnataka and Telangana, and said the weak monsoon is hurting sales of entry bikes, cars, appliances and everyday goods.

A sad local story shows the strain: a farmer in Nanded with a ₹10,000 loan was found dead in a private lender's office. Private lenders fill the gap where banks do not reach.

Shoppers spend less on cards

Card spending in September was about ₹2 lakh crore, down 6% from a year earlier. Quick lending apps and stricter rules, including the end of rent payments by card, pulled spending away.

  • Who gains — HDFC Bank raised its share to 32%; the top 5 banks hold about 79%.
  • Who loses — InCred rates SBI Cards "reduce" with a ₹520 target.

Trade deals and Europe's curbs on China
